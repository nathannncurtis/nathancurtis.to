# Burnt Orange — An Explication

*Some notes on a poem I wrote, and what I built into it.*

---

## What It Actually Is

This reads like a poem about a friendship, and it was built to read that way. It is a love poem.

I was in love with a close friend. Before I could tell her, minutes before, at the end of a night that was a date in everything but name, she got there first and set a "just friends" boundary, citing, among other things, the age gap between us. The friendship kept going. So did an emotional intimacy that never matched the word she'd put on it. I wasn't allowed to say the thing, so I wrote it instead.

I gave her the poem for her birthday, as the centerpiece of a gift that included a scavenger hunt I built for her. That delivery matters. A confession handed over as a birthday present can't be turned down without turning down a gift, and can't be acknowledged without breaking the boundary she set. The deniability is in the occasion, not just the language. I wasn't cornering her; I was giving her a way to take the intimacy without having to refuse the romance out loud. It cost me everything I had to give it that way, but that's what it was.

Some months later she moved away. Contact thinned, then stopped. The poem turned out to be exactly what I'd called it while writing it: a premortem. It forecast its own ending and hoped it was wrong.

---

## The Two Readings

Read cold, the poem is warm. Autumn, a falling leaf, a crow on a wire, seasons turning, two people changing alongside the weather, ending on an open-hearted note about a friendship still unfolding. That reading isn't wrong; it's the one I engineered, and the warmth in it is real. The hours it gestures at happened, and they were good.

But run the inventory. Changing leaves, a fallen leaf, autumn, winter, a spring that never comes, a fleeting moment, wind carrying things off, a fading sky, a bird leaving. Almost every image is movement away. The poem holds affection but is built around loss. If it leaves you faintly sad without telling you why, that's the architecture doing its job under the surface.

The honest version of the poem is both at once. It fights itself to hold warmth and grief in the same lines, and the fight loses ground stanza by stanza until the crow takes flight. That doubleness isn't a flaw or pure camouflage; it's me, telling the truth the only way I was allowed to.

---

## The Architecture

Four things I built in that don't show on a casual read.

**There is no "I" in the poem.** No first-person singular anywhere. No "she," either. No stated want. A love poem where the lover isn't permitted to exist as someone with desires; only "we" and "our," the grammar of the unit she defined. The poem obeys her boundary down to its pronouns. (This explication spends the "I" the poem refused to. That's the point of it.)

**Nature owns every verb of power.** The leaves danced and drifted; the seasons shifted; the wind carries. The people are objects being acted on, carried forward, navigating a path they didn't pave. If the stasis and the fading are just weather, then nobody chose them and nobody's to blame. The passivity is there to let me look away from the fact that a choice was made against me.

**She's barely in it.** For a poem about a person, there's almost no person: a first hello, smiles, shared glances, and otherwise landscape. Two reasons, both true. One, I write abstract by default; atmosphere is my register. Two, this had an audience of one; specifics like "the day we did X" would have decrypted the confession for the only reader who mattered. The abstraction is style and security at the same time.

**The title is the thesis.** Burnt orange is the transitional color, what's there when the green is dying but winter hasn't arrived. The whole poem lives in that in-between: not lovers, not strangers; not autumn, not winter; not arrival, not departure. Everything is almost becoming something else and never gets permission to. I chose the title for exactly that. The color is the relationship.

---

## The Images

**The single leaf.** "One, a single leaf, swayed, paused, before resting gently on the ground." It's isolated deliberately: one. It's the love, a single fragile thing watched as it falls, and it's her, the one who drifted down and away. I never resolve which, because it's both, and the "whisper of what's yet to come" is the premortem announcing itself in line five.

**The ember and the spring.** "A hidden ember beneath the frost, a quiet spring waiting, just out of reach." The ember is the feeling, kept alive under the frost of the boundary; hidden, obedient, quiet. But "just out of reach" is where the disguise inverts. Everywhere else I turn wanting into weather; here the weather is openly wanting. Seasons don't wait. Nothing is "out of reach" unless a hand is reaching and hitting a wall. It's the one moment I admit, plainly, to trying and failing to touch the thing I want, and I buried it in the second stanza so it could pass. Against the scavenger hunt, a game that is nothing but reaching for what's hidden, it's the worst of it: I led her through a maze of reaching to hand her a poem saying the real destination stays untouched.

**The crow.** The most patient image, and the most layered. It's a witness, the one still thing in a poem where everything moves, watching the "we" from above. It's also me: careful, contemplating, clutching a cold wire, watching my own life from a remove because living it directly wasn't allowed. The viewpoint dissociates up onto the powerline. And the crow doesn't belong in that sky, black against soft orange, an intruder in a warm scene, the way I didn't belong in the role I was playing. When it finally takes flight in the last stanza, that's the premortem executing: the watcher leaves because the scene can't be held anymore. The exit was always in there, waiting.

**The wind.** Gentle, whispering, and carrying them. Gentle doesn't cancel direction. It moves them through the seasons and, finally, apart, and it never asks. It's my honest verb for how the end actually came: not by anyone's stated decision, just by motion nobody interrupted.

**The ending.** "Something we're still discovering, together, in this ever-changing season of friendship." The last line is the weakest as craft, and I know why: it's the lie. Five stanzas lean toward a word I'm not allowed to say, and at the last second the poem says her word instead. The label, pasted on, getting the final say, literally. The poem is one cut from honest; end it on "something we're still discovering" and it stops holding her boundary for her. I left the lie in because you don't hand someone their eulogy on their birthday.

---

## The Line

The hinge of the whole poem is two lines in the fourth stanza: "subtle moments of almost-rhyme, but don't, and that's just right."

Seven words. This is the wound, where the confession comes closest to the surface, and it's the densest thing in the poem. The layers:

**1. The self-description.** The poem is free verse, full of near-misses and slant sounds that approach resolution and refuse it. The line names its own prosody. This is the visible layer, the alibi: I'm only describing the verse.

**2. Her voice, quoted.** "Don't," hanging with no subject, is grammatically a command, the only one in the poem, and it isn't mine. It's her boundary dropped into the text: we can be close, but don't. I quote her rule back to her inside the document that breaks its spirit.

**3. The anatomy of a slant rhyme.** An almost-rhyme, slant or half rhyme, is two sounds that share enough to make the ear expect resolution, then withhold it. The frustration is the point. As a metaphor for us it's exact: the matching parts there, the union withheld, the listener left wanting the landing. Not "we fit imperfectly." Closer to: we keep approaching resolution and getting denied it.

**4. The missing object.** "But don't," don't what? One way: the lines don't rhyme. The other: we don't. Don't resolve. Don't cross. Don't become the thing. The sentence trails off exactly where being specific would be dangerous. The grammar performs the suppression it describes; I couldn't finish the thought without exposing the wound, so I left it unfinished, on purpose, in plain sight.

**5. The cycle, performed.** The line is a whole act of self-censorship in one breath: observation (almost-rhyme, something is happening, there's a pattern, there's mutuality), retreat (but don't), justification (that's just right). Approach, withdraw, rationalize. Most of the poem reports feeling; this line enacts it, in real time, in the syntax.

**6. "Right," four ways.** Factually right: the lines really don't rhyme, alibi intact. Morally right: given the boundary and the gap she named, restraint was the proper thing, and I validate her logic even as it guts me. Resignedly right: the Goldilocks "just right," the verbal version of nodding when someone tells you no, with an edge of sarcasm in it, because nothing here was just right, and the overemphasis is the tell. And right as a question: repunctuate it, "that's just, right?", and "just" turns into fairness; the assertion becomes an appeal, me asking her and myself whether any of this is just. A question wearing acceptance as a costume.

**7. The phonetic performance.** Read it out loud. Pause after "rhyme." Listen to rhyme and right: shared vowel, divergent final consonant. A textbook slant rhyme. The line about almost-rhyming almost rhymes; it performs its own thesis on anyone who speaks it. It does what it says while denying that it's doing it. That was deliberate; this is the showcase line, and that's the flex.

**8. The origin command.** Right is a homophone. "That's just right" is "that's just write." The buried sentence: "...don't (say it), just write (the poem)." The line where the confession nearly surfaces contains, at its center, the instruction that made the poem exist. Don't say it. Just write. Four days, by hand, into a birthday gift. The thesis line is also its own writing prompt, and the poem is the evidence of the order being followed.

Put together: the line is the situation (her voice, the boundary, the almost), the response to the situation (the retreat, the compliance, the swallowed grief), and the instruction for how to respond (don't say it; just write); seven words demonstrating their own meaning out loud while denying it on the page. The poem was built around this line.

---

## Premortem

I called the poem three things while writing it: a show-off, a confession, and a premortem. All three are accurate.

The show-off is the line, and everything under it.

The confession is the whole thing, a love letter built to pass inspection, which it did, against readers who had no key.

The premortem is the ending I already knew. "A quiet spring waiting, just out of reach," not coming; waiting, watched from a distance. "The burnt orange fades." The crow takes flight. The poem hoped while it forecast, and the forecast won: she left, the contact thinned, and the friendship the last line insists on didn't survive the dynamic the poem documents. The "together" at the end was a wish when I wrote it and a ghost soon after. I let the illusion stand because it was a gift, and you don't hand someone their eulogy on their birthday. But the eulogy is in there, in the architecture, for anyone holding the key.

If you read it cold first and got a warm autumn poem: that reading was real. So is this one. It's both at once. It fights itself to hold both, and the fight, not the warmth and not the grief, is the reason it's worth your time.
