# Routine in Green — An Explication

*Some notes on a nervous tic that isn't mine, and the anxiety underneath it that is.*

---

## What It Actually Is

This reads like a poem about using a tube of mint lip balm until it runs out. It was built to pass as exactly that. It is a poem about anxiety: the chronic, low-grade, private kind, and the cheap repetitive mechanisms we lean on to hold it off.

One thing up front, because it's part of the point. The tic isn't mine. I don't compulsively use chapstick. I borrowed the habit as an object to route a real feeling through, the way you'd write grief through a house you never lived in. The anxiety is mine. The click is rented. That matters, because it means the disguise here goes a layer deeper than just dressing the feeling in imagery; I took someone else's nervous habit and encoded my own anxiety into the sound of it. If the poem works, it isn't because I confessed a habit. It's because the feeling underneath was real enough to survive being poured into a borrowed container.

---

## The Two Readings

The surface is tactile and safe: chapped lips, the twist of the tube, the sharp hit of mint, the small disappointment of running out. Relatable. Low-stakes.

The honest reading is psychological and uncomfortable. The "ache" is a chronic internal state, not dry skin. The "click click" is a metronome for a frantic mind, a way to impose order where there's none. The lips "cry their wear" from stress, from the tongue worrying at them, from the teeth. It's a portrait of a coping mechanism wrung out until it's empty, and the recognition that the mechanism was always going to run dry while the thing it was soothing stays exactly where it was.

But the real work of this poem isn't in the imagery. It's in the sound. This is a poem you have to perform with your mouth to read, and performing it makes you enact the very tic it describes.

---

## The Sound

Four things the poem does phonetically that don't show on the page, only in the mouth.

**The dental enactment.** "The temptation of tongue, the dread of the teeth." Say it slowly. Every stressed consonant is a T, TH, or D, the sounds you make by tapping your tongue against or just behind your teeth. To articulate a line about the tongue and the teeth, your tongue has to repeatedly strike your teeth. The line doesn't describe the anxious mouth tic; it makes you perform it while reading it.

**The click is a mouth sound.** A click isn't only a plastic tube; it's something the mouth makes, a tongue-click, the little tut of an anxious person. Sitting right beside "tongue" and "teeth," the click stops being purely the object and fuses with a vocal tic. The sound of the coping device and the sound of the anxious body become the same sound.

**The hard K spine, hidden in C.** Listen for the K running through the whole poem: cliCk, Creases, craCk, Cheap, Cost, Callous, cliCk. The percussive K is the poem's pulse, the tic bleeding into the vocabulary even where the tube isn't present. But here's the concealment: that hard sound is almost always spelled with a C, the soft, common, native English letter, not the rarer, harder K. The percussion is real in the ear and hidden in the spelling. The hard thing wearing soft clothing. The K sealed inside the C, the same way everything hard in this poem is sealed inside something soft.

**The click that departs.** "Click click" appears five times. In the first four stanzas it's a hard onset, leading its line, arriving, starting things. Then once, only once, it changes: "As the click click grows distant." Here it's demoted out of the lead position, buried in a subordinate clause, cushioned by soft sibilants on both sides ("As the" in front, "grows distant" behind) so the hard click is muffled. Every other click arrives; this one leaves. That's the rhythm failing in real time. The beat that organizes the whole poem recedes at exactly the point the speaker is either calming down or coming apart, and the grammar enacts the recession by stripping the click of its power and softening its edges. Then it returns in the next stanza as "a callous click click," hardened and numb, and the poem ends alone on "just for me." Arrives, arrives, arrives, arrives, fades, returns deadened, ends isolated.

---

## The Images

**The mask of mint.** The mint doesn't cure the ache; it overrides the channel with a sharper, colder, more immediate signal. "An ache masked in flavor" up top, "the mint soothes the pain" at the bottom. The green is camouflage. Freshness masking decay. The flavor is a distraction wearing the costume of a remedy, which is the whole relationship between the routine and the anxiety in miniature.

**The dead tube.** "No twist left, wasting." The pacifier runs out, the threads hit bottom, the raw nerve is about to be exposed again. "Wasting" does triple work: the tube is used up, the speaker is wasting away, and the time spent treating the symptom instead of the disease is wasted. The dread of the empty tube is the dread of the mechanism failing while the need is still there.

**The passive relief.** "The mint approaches." The speaker doesn't reach for relief; it advances on him, on a schedule, like something owed. Throughout the poem he is acted upon more than acting, the relief comes to him, the lips cry, the thumb plays the rhythm almost on its own. The only thing he actively does is click, and even that reads as compulsion rather than choice.

---

## The Line

The hinge of the whole poem is one line in the fifth stanza: "Balm seals the creases, a crack no more."

I built six distinct meanings into it. They run simultaneously.

**1. The literal.** The balm fills the chapped, cracked lip. The crack is healed, gone. Plain repair.

**2. The face.** "Creases" as worry lines. Sealing the creases is smoothing visible distress, putting on a composed face so nothing shows. The balm conceals that anything is wrong.

**3. The sealed lips.** The balm is on the lips; sealed lips mean silence, secrecy, saying nothing. The anxiety is kept private and unspoken, which lands directly on "just for me" at the end. The coping isn't just lonely; it's mute.

**4. The breakdown.** "Crack" as cracking up, cracking under pressure. "A crack no more" then reads as holding it together, the psychological crack instead of the lip crack.

**5. "Seals" as traps, not heals.** To seal can mean to close something in. The balm seals the pain beneath the surface rather than curing it. A sealed crack is hidden, still there, just covered. Containment posing as treatment, symptom over disease. This is the poem's thesis in one verb.

**6. Balm itself, doing triple duty.** This is the deep one. "Balm" is three words at once. It's the literal lip product. It's *balm* as solace, the abstract noun, balm for the soul, comfort itself, which floats the whole line up off the chapstick into pure metaphor: solace seals the creases. And it's a homophone for *bomb*. "Balm" has a silent L; it's pronounced the same as "bomb." So heard aloud, "balm seals the creases" is also "bomb seals the creases," and now the line is armed: the thing that soothes and the thing that detonates are the same sound. A sealed bomb is a contained detonation, pressure held in, which is exactly what anxiety is, the calm is the lid on it, not the absence of it. The silent L matters here too: "balm" is spelled with a consonant you can't hear, carrying a detonation you don't show.

So the line, fully loaded: solace, or a bomb, seals (heals and traps) the creases (lip cracks and worry lines), a crack (lip split, breakdown, the snap of detonation) no more (healed, don't break again, silenced). The hard K of "crack" is the sound the bomb doesn't get to make because it's sealed. The crack is the detonation held in check.

And the sound of the line performs the meaning. "Balm seals the creases" is built from soft continuants, the L silent, the phrase smooth and sealed. Then "a crack" punches in with two hard K's, the word cracking the smoothness. Then "no more" softens again. The line sonically seals a crack: hardness bracketed by softness on both sides.

---

## The Last Line

"Click click, just for me."

"Just for me" is a slant. It sets up the expectation of warmth, a treat, a small kindness, this one's just for me, self-care, indulgence. That's the reading the ear reaches for. It lands one consonant off, on isolation and on plea: only me, nobody else, no one to see, let me have this. The phrase almost-rhymes with comfort and resolves on loneliness instead, and the gap between the two is the whole feeling. "Just" is the pivot and it won't settle: it can mean merely (diminishing, pathetic, only this), or justly, rightfully (this is owed to me), or be near-filler. The comforting surface reading and the desolate true reading occupy the identical four words.

---

## What It Is

Every layer of this poem is the same single gesture, repeated at every level: something hard and dangerous sealed inside something soft and common. The K inside the C. The bomb inside the balm. The detonation inside the comfort. The breakdown inside the chapstick routine. The plea inside "just for me." The real anxiety inside a borrowed tic. That gesture is what anxiety actually feels like from the inside: the calm surface is not the absence of the thing, it's the lid on it.

"Balm seals the creases" isn't a line in this poem. It's the poem's whole thesis about itself, performed six ways in seven words. And the fact that I built all of it through a habit that isn't even mine is the point, not a flaw in it. The feeling didn't need to be my habit. It just needed to be my feeling, sealed under enough soft surface to say out loud.
