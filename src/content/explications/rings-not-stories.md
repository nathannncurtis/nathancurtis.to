# Rings, not Stories — An Explication

*Some notes on being lost in the dark, the arrogance of wanting a plot, and a few old stories buried in single words.*

---

## What It Actually Is

This reads like a poem about a night walk in the woods that gets a little turned around. It was built to read that way. It is a poem about being lost: not in a forest, but in a life, the slow un-becoming of a person who can't answer who they are, and the indifference of the world to our need to be the main character.

The whole poem turns on one structural fact, so I'll say it first: you can't tell the walker is lost on the first read. The first time through, it's atmospheric, a little eerie, mostly calm. The trees seem benign. Only the final line, standing alone, reveals that the walker has been circling, and sends you back to the top. On the second read, knowing they're lost, everything recolors. The laughter becomes mockery. The family becomes the maze closing. That two-state design, innocent then horrifying, with the last line as the switch, is the engine. The poem is a ring. It makes you walk it twice, which means it makes you do what the walker does.

---

## The Two Readings

Read cold: a person walks through humid woods at night, hears a mouse dart off, an owl call, wind from a valley, passes ancient trees and exposed rock, and ends on a mild flicker of déjà vu.

Run the inventory and it inverts. "To what, to where?" undercuts any purpose in the third line. The mouse flees in terror. The owl's call echoes and goes unanswered. The wind is shouting. The trees are laughing with lungs full of sap. The environment isn't peaceful; it's an audience to the walker's lostness. And "Have I seen that one before?" isn't a quaint observation. It's the stomach-drop of realizing you've been walking in circles. The landscape is a maze wearing the costume of a forest.

---

## The Subject, Hidden in the First Word

"It's unbecoming; just walking."

Unbecoming does three things. It means improper, undignified, walking with no destination is beneath you, embarrassing. It means un-becoming, the reversal of becoming, you are coming undone, losing the self rather than growing one, which is what being lost in a life actually is. And it carries the participle problem that runs through the whole poem: becoming is an *-ing* word, ongoing, never finished, and the poem is full of *-ing*, walking, rustling, scurrying, running, listening, laughing, dancing, all this continuous motion that never completes, never reaches past tense, never *becomes* a finished act. The walker is stuck in the present participle, in becoming, never arriving at having-become. The poem tells you in line one that the walker has no answer to the question it's about to ask.

The word for the subject, "lost," never appears. The poem performs it instead of naming it.

---

## It Is Night, and That Is the Whole Problem

The poem never says "night." It proves it.

The owl proves it: owls are nocturnal, an owl active and calling means darkness. The perception proves it: the walker registers the owl by sound, "a faint flutter, a whisper of wings," not by sight, because there's nothing to see. The ecosystem proves it: nocturnal hunter, nocturnal prey, a food chain that only runs at night. Being lost in the woods at night is the real terror, the visual world is gone, your bearings are gone with it. That's why the compass fails and why the senses muddle. You're navigating by the wrong senses in the dark.

And the light source matters. In the old cave story the shadows are cast by a fire. Here they're cast by the moon. Different light, same deception.

---

## The Buried Words

This is the poem's deepest device, and it's why it can't be cracked on a casual read. Several single words are trapdoors. They read as ordinary nouns and contain entire other things.

**Pine.** "Pine muddles the senses." Reads as the tree, the scent. It's also *pine*, the verb, to long, to yearn. Longing muddles the senses. The buried word foreshadows the explicit "yearning" later in the poem and names the actual disease: it isn't the woods disorienting the walker, it's wanting. And it does triple duty, because Echo, below, pined herself away. Pine is a tree, a yearning, and a myth.

**Who.** "A 'who' echoes, alone." It's the owl's call, but it's printed "who," the question, not "hoo," the sound. Spelled as the question, it tells you the owl was never just a bird; it's the question *who are you* wearing feathers. The identity question, asked in the dark, returning as an echo with no answer.

**Again.** "The granite lies again exposed." *Again* is the tell for recurrence: this has happened before, it will happen again, the same thing exposed over and over, which is the walker's circling at geological scale and the whole poem's looping structure in one word.

**Granite.** The deepest trapdoor. See below.

---

## The Granite

"While the granite lies again exposed."

On the surface: bedrock uncovered by erosion, deep time, the ancient indifferent landscape against which the walker's crisis is nothing. "Lies" doubles, the granite *rests* exposed and the granite *deceives*, threading with the disorientation. Granite is the bedrock self getting uncovered as the surface erodes; you dig to the foundation hoping for solid ground, for an answer to "who," and the bedrock lies too. There's no honest floor. Which is the whole poem refusing to bottom out.

But the granite carries two old stories, and a careful reader gets them from more than the rock alone.

**The cave.** The walk opens with "a shadow scurries across the path," and the walker tries to name it, "just a rat, a mouse maybe," reading the shadow, unable to tell what casts it. That's the old allegory of the cave: prisoners mistaking shadows for the real. The walker is in the cave, at night, naming shadows cast by the moon, wanting to ascend to the real thing, the true narrative, the meaning, and trapped reading shapes in the dark. The breadcrumbs are the shadow, the darkness, and the inability to see real things, not the granite alone.

**Sisyphus.** The rock worn by being exposed and buried and exposed *again*, the valley implying the hill, the trees that "continue dancing, like they always do," the repetition everywhere. This is the absurd, the boulder rolled up and rolling back forever. The walker is Sisyphus, circling, arriving nowhere, the loop resetting. And the prescription of that story, one must imagine Sisyphus happy, is what the trees have achieved and the walker hasn't. The trees are content with rings, content to do the same thing forever without it going anywhere; they chuckle, they dance, they've made peace with the loop. The walker wants a novel, a destination, and suffers it. The trees are Sisyphus happy. The walker is Sisyphus refusing. The breadcrumbs are "again," the valley, and the dancing "like they always do," not the granite alone.

---

## Echo, in the Valley

"The wind shouting from the valley afar."

A voice you can't make out, calling from far off, in a valley, returning. That's Echo, condemned to never speak first and only repeat, who pined for Narcissus until her body wasted and only her voice was left, haunting the valleys. The poem seeds her in several places: the valley shout, the word *echoes* itself in "a 'who' echoes," the self-repetition of "still walking" returning as "just walking," and the looping final line that forces the reader to repeat the whole poem, becoming Echo. And "pine," again, because Echo pined herself to nothing. The yearning that muddles the senses is also her specific longing.

And Narcissus comes with her. He fell for his own reflection, couldn't tell image from real, wasted away staring at himself, which rhymes with the cave's shadow-deception and with the owl below. "Have I seen that one before?" is the walker circling back to their own traces, encountering themselves, the way Narcissus did.

---

## The Owl

The owl is the center of the poem, and it is never explicitly called an owl. The flutter, the wings, the "who," the nocturnal hunt imply it. It's an owl for reasons.

It's an owl because an owl means night; it's the timestamp.

It follows the walker, and the poem gives the reason: an owl shadows a large creature moving through brush because the movement flushes prey, mice, voles, insects. The walker beats the bush; the owl hunts what the walker kicks up. So the owl isn't lost alongside the walker; it's *hunting* alongside the walker, treating the walker as a tool. The walker's pointless, exhausting circling is productive, just not for the walker. Your lost, meaningless motion feeds the thing that's following you. That's why it's the same owl at the start and the end: it never left, because the walker is its meal-ticket. "Have I seen that one before?", yes, it's been with you since the third stanza.

And the owl is the walker. As the walker un-becomes, comes apart, the self that asks "who" splits off and follows from outside, the dissociated self, watching and hunting the scraps the falling-apart kicks up. That's why the question "who" comes from the owl: it's the part of the walker that no longer recognizes the body it left. This is what makes "unbecoming" land in full. The self separates out into the owl. You come undone, and the part that asks who you are detaches and follows you through the dark.

The trees don't ask "who." They're at peace. The walker's owl won't let them be, because the walker, unlike the trees, can't stop needing the answer.

---

## The Other Images

**The rat and the mouse.** "Just a rat, a mouse maybe." The walker can't identify a common thing and won't commit. "Maybe" is the sound of a mind that no longer trusts its own perception, adding doubt to everything because certainty is gone. It plants the self-doubt that the final line pays off: the walker has been unreliable to themselves the whole way.

**The trees.** "They're listening, laughing. But they do not speak, nor cast judgment. For they cannot, with their lungs filled with sap." An audience that perceives but can't pronounce. They can't judge the walker, not from mercy but from incapacity, choked silent by their own lifeblood. They "cannot breathe alone, yet they persevere," rooted, connected, enduring without needing a story. They model a way of being the walker can't access. Everything that could tell the walker who they are is silent; the only thing that speaks is the owl, the thing that *asks*.

**Novels, not rings, and where novels come from.** "Their yearning's not for novels, but for rings." Novels are linear, plot, beginning-middle-end, meaning, a story that goes somewhere. Rings are circular, additive, another year survived, no plot, just accretion in place. A ring records *that* you survived, not *how* you felt; it's meaning-free survival. The human wants the story; the tree wants only to become more of itself where it stands. And it puns: the walker wants a novel but is walking in *rings*, the shape they're trapped in is the shape the trees are content with. But the sick part is where novels come from. Novels are paper. Paper is pulped trees. To become a novel, a tree has to be killed and ground up. So the trees don't yearn for novels because becoming one means dying into it. The walker's craving for a narrative is, from the tree's side, a death wish, a desire to be cut down and made legible. To want a novel is to want to be made dead and readable. To want rings is to stay alive and illegible.

**The moss.** "The moss now looks west." Moss grows north; that's fact, and the poem isn't claiming otherwise. The question is why the walker reads it as west, and the answer is that the walker has rotated ninety degrees without feeling it. North looks like west because you've quarter-turned and your internal compass didn't update. The moss is honest. The walker is turned around and can't feel the turning. Being lost isn't the world deceiving you; it's you being mis-oriented and not knowing it. And what turned the walker is the owl: you turn toward a sound in the dark, toward the call, toward the "who," and turning to face the question of who you are is exactly what knocked you off course.

**The grain.** "A new grin on the same grain." Same wood, same tree, new expression. The same landmark wearing a new face each loop, because you can't hold it. The tree's nature is fixed (same grain) and its look shifts (new grin), the inverse of the walker, whose face is fixed and whose nature, whose grain, whose answer to "who," is gone.

**The timber.** "All the timber gathers near." Timber is dead wood, felled trees, the word for trees once cut for use, including paper, including novels. The walker who wanted novels is surrounded at the end by *timber*, the dead-tree version, the novel-material, gathering like family. To get your story you have to die into it; family is the timber because the welcome is into deadness-made-legible. It reads as comfort and it's the maze closing.

---

## The Last Line

"Have I seen that one before?"

Standing alone, off the body of the poem.

Surface: déjà vu, that tree looks familiar. Real: the walker is circling and has just half-recognized a landmark they already passed. The trap springs in the last line.

And "that one" has a correct answer, and it's the owl. The "who" you keep circling back to, the one that's followed you since the third stanza, the shed self, the question of your own identity wearing feathers. Have I seen that one before. Yes. You've been orbiting it the whole walk; it's the axis of the circle, the constant while everything else seems to shift, and you can't get away from the thing you're spinning around. The answer is yes, and the poem makes you prove it by sending you back to the top to re-walk the ring, which makes *you* the one who's seen it before now, too.

---

## What It Is

Every reassuring word in this poem is a trapdoor. Pine is yearning. Who is a person, not a bird. Again is recurrence. Granite is the cave and the boulder. The valley is Echo. The owl is your own shed self, following you to feed on what your coming-apart kicks up. The family is the maze. The compass is honest and you are the one who's turned.

And under all of it is a single subject refracted through every myth and device: lost, un-becoming, circling a self you can't face, in the dark, forever, while the rooted things around you have made their peace with the same loop and you can't. The poem hides that it's about being lost until the last line throws you back to the start and makes you Echo, re-walking the circle to find that the woods were never kind. It is the most over-built thing I've made, and the density isn't decoration. It's the point. You can read it a hundred times and there's another trapdoor under the floor you thought was the bottom.

Have you seen that one before? Read it again. You have now.
